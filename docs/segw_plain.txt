Abstract. Recent large-scale world models focus on generating plausible interactive environments, while generalist agents emphasize task
execution across diverse worlds. We study a complementary problem:
how can a world model self-refine its understanding of environment mechanics through interaction? We propose a self-evolving world model
that performs online system identification in a game environment with
initially unknown physics. Rather than merely predicting next states,
the model extracts structured constraints such as maximum jumpable
height and collision tolerances and iteratively updates these hypotheses using observed interaction traces. We instantiate this framework using large language models as the reasoning backbone and evaluate in
a procedurally generated platforming domain with hidden physical parameters. Our experiments demonstrate that the model progressively reduces parameter estimation error, sharpens feasibility boundary prediction, improves agent completion rates, and generates solvable levels with
high precision. Furthermore, under abrupt physics changes, the model
rapidly re-identifies altered dynamics, significantly outperforming static
or black-box baselines. These results suggest that interactive constraint
refinement provides a principled pathway toward adaptive and interpretable world modeling. Our code, and training setup will be publicly
released.

Introduction

Understanding and modeling the dynamics of interactive environments is a central challenge in computer vision, artificial intelligence, and simulation research.
Traditional world modeling approaches have pursued two largely orthogonal objectives. One line of work focuses on foundation generative world models, which
learn broad priors over environment structure and dynamics from large offline
corpora [2, 13]. These models can generate rich, plausible simulations of interactive worlds conditioned on text, images, or other modalities, enabling highfidelity visual synthesis and controllable scenario generation. Such foundation
models excel at producing plausible worlds, but they are not explicitly designed
to discover and refine the true laws and constraints that govern any particular
environment encountered in deployment. Their learned priors do not adaptively
ground themselves in environment-specific physics, limiting their usefulness for
tasks requiring precise, environment-specific dynamics.

or generalist agents that optimize for task performance, SEGW’s objective is to
iteratively infer and formalize the true environmental laws that define feasibility,
dynamics, and interaction boundaries within a fixed engine.

Recent progress in large-scale world modeling has produced systems that synthesize rich environments from large offline corpora using powerful generative
backbones. A prominent line of work focuses on foundation world models that
learn broad priors over interactive environments and can generate plausible trajectories or scenes conditioned on prompts, exemplified by Genie and its successors [?, 2]. In parallel, several systems couple large language models with professional game engines to generate controllable interactive 3D worlds from natural
language. WorldCraft uses LLM agents to compose photo-realistic scenes and
assets with controllable layout and object properties [12], while LatticeWorld
and related Unreal-based pipelines target scalable interactive world production
by combining LLM planning with engine-native scene generation and simulation
tools [4, 11]. Related efforts also explore text-to-interactive 3D world generation
and editing loops with engine integration [19].
While these systems improve usability and controllability of content creation,
their primary objective is typically plausible generation rather than environmentspecific law discovery. Mechanics are largely implicit encoded in engine behavior,
heuristics, or pretrained priors and are not maintained as a structured hypothesis
that can be queried, tightened, or revised online. In contrast, SEGW operates
in a fixed engine setting and treats interaction as the principal inference-time
signal for refining an explicit set of feasibility constraints, which is critical for
calibrated solvability prediction and feasibility-aware procedural generation.

Embodied generalist agents [3, 15] focus on learning policies that act competently across diverse environments, often leveraging implicit world models to
support planning and control; however, these representations are optimized for
task performance rather than for explicitly identifying environment laws or feasibility boundaries. Procedural content generation (PCG) methods [?, 18] similarly exploit learned latent spaces or evolutionary search to produce diverse and
playable levels, but they typically optimize for novelty or playability metrics
without maintaining an interpretable model of the underlying mechanics that
govern feasibility. In parallel, online and inference-time refinement frameworks
in LLMs [?,16,20] demonstrate that iterative feedback and memory can improve
outputs without parameter updates, yet they target answer refinement or policy behavior rather than structured environment identification. In contrast to
these lines of work, SEGW treats interaction as a signal for explicit constraint
discovery: instead of adapting a policy (embodied agents), optimizing content
quality (PCG), or refining textual outputs (LLM feedback loops), our framework
incrementally constructs and revises a structured, queryable hypothesis over environment mechanics. This distinction enables calibrated feasibility estimation,
boundary contraction toward true engine limits, and rapid adaptation under
physics shifts capabilities that are not central objectives in embodied control,
generative PCG, or inference-time language refinement paradigms. By unifying
system identification principles with structured memory and interactive generation, SEGW advances world modeling from implicit representation learning to
explicit, interpretable constraint evolution.

Problem Formulation

We consider a deterministic interactive environment E governed by unknown
engine parameters θ∗ ∈ Θ, which define latent mechanics such as gravity, movement limits, collision tolerances, or resource dynamics. At time step t, the environment state st ∈ S evolves according to the unknown transition dynamics
st+1 = fθ∗ (st , at ). The agent in our framework serves solely as an interaction
probe. Unlike standard reinforcement learning setups, our objective is not to
learn a policy π(a|s) that maximizes cumulative reward, but rather to infer a
structured hypothesis Ht ≈ θ∗ that encodes explicit feasibility constraints governing valid transitions and current game level solvability.
After each episode, structured evidence Et is extracted from gameplay trajectories, including boundary interactions, failure cases, and successful transitions. The hypothesis is updated via an inference-time refinement operator:
Ht = U(Ht−1 , Et ), where U denotes the structured memory update mechanism
that adds, revises, reweights, or archives constraint rules.

and collision tolerances. Levels contain structured platform layouts, enemies, and
gap configurations. This domain evaluates jump-boundary estimation, traversal
feasibility, and difficulty pacing constraints.

A — Foundation Prior (Static LLM). This baseline represents large-scale generative world models that rely on pretrained priors without environment-specific
refinement. A large language model (e.g., GPT-style or Gemini-style model)
is prompted once with environment descriptions and level summaries, but no
persistent memory or hypothesis update mechanism is maintained. Predictions
therefore depend entirely on pretrained knowledge and contextual reasoning. Example: Estimating whether a jump is feasible based solely on textual reasoning
about typical gravity values, without incorporating interaction feedback from
failed attempts.
B — Latent Recurrent World Model (RSSM). This baseline represents modelbased reinforcement learning approaches that learn implicit neural dynamics
models. We implement a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) trained to minimize multi-step rollout prediction error, similar to open-source PlaNet and
Dreamer-style architectures. The model learns a compact latent representation
of environment transitions and supports predictive planning; however, it does
not maintain explicit feasibility constraints or structured boundary representations. Example: Accurately predicting future states in latent space while being
unable to explicitly report the maximum jumpable height or precise collision
threshold.

C — SEGW (Ours). Our proposed framework maintains an explicit structured
rule memory Ht that encodes mechanical and semantic constraints. These rules
are iteratively refined through structured evidence extraction and contradictiondriven updates. Example: Observing repeated jump failures near a gap boundary
and tightening the inferred maximum jump height constraint accordingly.
Together, these three variants enable a principled comparison between (i)
static prior reasoning, (ii) implicit latent predictive modeling, and (iii) explicit
constraint-based system identification. This setup directly tests our central hypothesis: that structured feasibility memory yields sharper boundary estimation,
improved calibration, and faster adaptation than either static priors or purely
predictive neural world models.

Conclusion

We introduced Self-Evolving Game Worlds (SEGW), a structured world modeling framework that reframes environment understanding as online constraint
discovery rather than next-state prediction or policy optimization. By maintaining explicit, interpretable feasibility rules and refining them through interactiondriven evidence, SEGW achieves substantially lower parameter and boundary estimation error, improved calibration, faster adaptation under physics shifts, and
stronger downstream agent performance across navigation, platforming, and survival domains. Compared to static foundation priors and latent recurrent world
models, structured constraint refinement yields sharper feasibility boundaries
and more reliable planning behavior. These results demonstrate that explicit
rule memory and contradiction-driven updates provide a principled and scalable
pathway toward adaptive, interpretable world models for interactive systems.
